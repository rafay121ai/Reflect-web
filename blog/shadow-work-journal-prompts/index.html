40 shadow work journal prompts for what you usually look away from

Updated August 12, 2026

Shadow work is a term borrowed from Jungian psychology for a simple idea: the parts of yourself you find hardest to admit do not disappear just because you stopped looking at them. They show up sideways, as a reaction that is bigger than the moment deserves, a trait you cannot stand in someone else, or a version of yourself you perform so consistently you forget it is a performance.

None of that requires a diagnosis or a dramatic reveal. Most shadow work is quieter than the name suggests: noticing a pattern, writing it down honestly, and asking where it came from instead of where it is aimed. The prompts below are built to start there, not at some imagined rock bottom.

A caution worth stating plainly: journaling can surface old material, and that is normal. If a prompt brings up something that feels bigger than a page can hold, that is a signal to talk to a therapist, not a reason to push through alone.

Triggers and reactions

An outsized reaction is information. These prompts start with the reaction and work backward.

  1. What is something small that made me disproportionately angry recently, and why might it have hit harder than it should have?
  2. Whose comment or tone can put me in a bad mood for hours, and what does it remind me of?
  3. When did I last feel humiliated, and what exactly about it stung?
  4. What kind of criticism do I have almost no patience for, even when it is fair?
  5. What situation makes me want to leave the room before I have even decided to?
  6. When have I overreacted and known it in the moment, but reacted anyway?
  7. What do I assume people mean when they go quiet around me?
  8. What is a reaction of mine that people who know me well could probably predict?

The goal is not to excuse the reaction. It is to trace it back to what it is actually protecting.

What I judge in others

Traits that bother you in other people are often traits you have disowned in yourself, or permission you have not given yourself to have. These prompts work best without softening the answer.

  1. What trait in other people irritates me far more than it should?
  2. Who do I quietly envy, and what specifically do they have that I want?
  3. What kind of person do I judge fastest, before I know anything else about them?
  4. Is there a version of that trait, even a small one, that exists in me?
  5. What do I criticize in others that I have done myself, in a different form?
  6. Whose confidence bothers me, and what would it cost me to be that direct?
  7. What do I call "too much" in someone else that I have learned to suppress in myself?
  8. What success in someone else makes me feel smaller, and why?

If an honest answer feels uncomfortable, that discomfort is usually the point, not a sign to stop.

What I hide or perform

Most people keep a version of themselves for public use. These prompts look at the gap between that version and the one underneath.

  1. What do I let people believe about me that is not quite true?
  2. What would surprise the people who think they know me well?
  3. What do I perform competence about, even when I feel unsure underneath?
  4. What have I never said out loud to anyone, not because it is shameful, but because it feels too exposed?
  5. When do I laugh along instead of saying what I actually think?
  6. What identity do I hold onto because dropping it feels like losing something, even if it no longer fits?
  7. What part of myself do I only show to one person, or to no one?
  8. What am I afraid people would think of me if they saw the unedited version?

Naming the performance does not mean abandoning it overnight. It just makes it a choice again instead of a reflex.

Where it started

Patterns usually have an origin. These prompts trace beliefs about yourself back to where they were learned, not to assign blame, but to see the belief clearly enough to question it.

  1. What was I taught, directly or indirectly, about showing anger growing up?
  2. What did I learn I had to do to be loved or approved of as a child?
  3. What emotion was treated as unacceptable in my household?
  4. Whose voice do I hear when I criticize myself?
  5. What rule did I make about myself early on that I have never actually reexamined?
  6. What did I have to become in order to feel safe in my family?
  7. What did I decide about my worth after a specific event, and is that decision still active?
  8. What did the adults around me avoid feeling, and did I learn to avoid it too?

A belief formed at seven does not have to still be true at thirty. Naming its origin is what makes it possible to update.

Integration

The point of shadow work is not to dislike what you find. It is to bring disowned parts back into a fuller, more honest picture of yourself.

  1. What disowned trait, once I look at it honestly, has also helped me somewhere in my life?
  2. What would it look like to admit a flaw out loud to someone I trust?
  3. What part of myself am I ready to stop apologizing for?
  4. What would change if I treated a "bad" trait as information instead of evidence against me?
  5. Where do I need less performance and more honesty, even if it is less impressive?
  6. What have I forgiven in someone else that I have not yet forgiven in myself?
  7. What would the most integrated version of me, flaws included, actually look like day to day?
  8. What is one small, specific way I can act differently this week based on what I have noticed here?

Integration is not a single breakthrough. It is a series of smaller honest moments, most of which look unremarkable from the outside.

How to use these without spiraling

Pick one prompt, not five. Shadow work goes further with one honest page than with a rushed survey of every question here. Write the first true answer, not the most flattering one, and resist editing it into something more comfortable before you have even finished the sentence.

Give yourself an end point. Ten or fifteen minutes is enough for most of these. If a prompt opens something larger than journaling can hold, that is worth bringing to a therapist rather than working through alone on a page.

FAQ

Is shadow work the same as therapy?

No. Shadow work is a self-reflection practice, not a clinical treatment, and it works well alongside therapy but is not a substitute for it, especially for trauma or clinical mental health conditions.

How often should I do shadow work journaling?

Weekly is usually more sustainable than daily. These prompts ask for honesty that is hard to sustain every day without it becoming rote or exhausting.

What if I do not like what I find?

That reaction is common and is not a sign you are doing it wrong. Noticing a trait honestly is different from acting on it, and the goal is awareness, not immediate change.
